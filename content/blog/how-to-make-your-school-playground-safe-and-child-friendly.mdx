A school playground is used harder than almost any other play area. Hundreds of children, short breaks, everyone rushing out at once. Safety here is not only about good equipment; it is about how the space is planned and run every day.

These seven steps are the ones we work through with schools when we plan a new playground or upgrade an old one.

## 1. Divide the playground by age

What is safe and fun for a 12-year-old is risky for a 3-year-old, and what suits a 3-year-old bores a 12-year-old into climbing the outside of the structure. Start with zones:

<Table caption="Typical zones in a school playground" head={["Zone","Ages","Suitable equipment"]} rows={[["Pre-primary","3 to 6","Low play stations, 3 ft slides, spring riders, crawl tunnels, play panels, sand play"],["Primary","6 to 10","Multi play stations with 5 ft slides, climbers, balance beams, swings, see-saws"],["Older children","10 and above","Taller climbing nets, adventure structures, rotating climbers, fitness-style bars"]]} />

Separate the zones with a path, low planting or a change in surface colour so children understand where they belong. Read more in [designing age-appropriate zones](/blog/designing-age-appropriate-zones-in-outdoor-playgrounds.html).

## 2. Choose equipment built for heavy use

School equipment should be designed to a recognised safety standard (most Indian suppliers use EN 1176), with galvanised and powder-coated steel, UV-stabilised roto-moulded plastic, rounded edges and no gaps that can trap heads or fingers. Ask the supplier for the fall height and age range of every unit. Our guide to [playground safety standards in India](/blog/playground-safety-standards-in-india.html) explains what to check.

## 3. Put a safe surface under everything

Concrete, paver blocks and compacted soil cause serious injuries even from low falls. Under every piece of equipment, and across its whole safety zone, use rubber tiles, poured rubber, artificial turf over a shock pad, or deep sand. Compare them in [school playground surface materials](/blog/school-playground-surface-materials-which-one-is-best.html).

## 4. Lay it out for movement and supervision

- **Keep swings at the edge**, away from the routes children run along.
- **Give slide exits a clear run-out**, not facing a wall, a bench or another unit.
- **Leave open ground** for running and chasing games, so they do not happen on the structures.
- **Make the whole area visible** from where staff stand on duty. Avoid tall structures that hide a corner.
- **Fence the play area** from parking, gates and driveways.

## 5. Plan for crowding

The biggest difference between a school and a park is the rush at break time. If one structure serves the whole primary section, children queue, push and fall. Spread play value across several pieces of equipment, add ground-level activities such as hopping posts and play panels, and stagger break times between sections if needed.

## 6. Shade, water and rules

Indian summers make metal and dark plastic hot to touch. Shade sails, trees on the south and west sides and light-coloured slides help. Keep drinking water close by. Put up a short, pictorial rules board at child height, in the school's languages, and teach the rules in the first week of term. See our [10 playground rules for kids](/blog/playground-safety-dos-and-donts-for-kids.html).

<Tip title="Get children involved" text="Ask a class to design the playground rules poster. Children remember rules they helped write, and the poster ends up in the language they actually use." />

## 7. Inspect and maintain

Name one person responsible for the playground. Give them a simple routine: a quick look every morning, a hands-on check of bolts, chains and surfaces every month, and a full inspection by the supplier once a year. Close damaged equipment the same day. Our [playground safety inspection checklist](/blog/playground-safety-inspection-checklist.html) is ready to print.
