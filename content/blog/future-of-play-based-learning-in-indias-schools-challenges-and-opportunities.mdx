The National Education Policy 2020 placed play- and activity-based learning at the heart of the foundational stage. Turning that into everyday practice in India's schools is a big task, with real obstacles and real opportunities.

## The challenges

### Teacher training

Guided play is a skill. Many teachers trained in lecture-based methods need support to plan and run play-based lessons.

### Space

City schools often have little outdoor space, and what exists may be concrete or used for assembly.

### Class sizes

Large classes make hands-on activities harder to manage.

### Expectations

Many parents expect early reading and writing results and see play as time wasted.

### Assessment

Exams reward memorisation; play-based learning develops skills that are harder to test.

## The opportunities

- **Policy support** gives schools permission to change.
- **Better outdoor spaces:** even small playgrounds can carry learning elements. See [playground design ideas for small spaces](/blog/playground-design-ideas-for-small-spaces.html).
- **Low-cost materials:** much play-based learning needs creativity more than money.
- **Communities and CSR** can fund play spaces in under-resourced schools.

## Practical next steps for schools

1. Start with the foundational classes.
2. Train a few teachers deeply, then share.
3. Use the outdoor space every day, not only at break.
4. Explain the approach to parents with examples of what children learn.
5. Improve the playground step by step. See [budget-friendly playground equipment for schools](/blog/budget-friendly-playground-equipment-options-for-schools-in-india.html).

## What a play-based school looks like

In a school that has embraced play-based learning, you might see:

- Pre-primary children spending part of every morning in a well-equipped outdoor area, with teachers guiding play towards counting, language and movement goals.
- Primary classes taking maths and science outside once or twice a week.
- A playground with variety for every age, used for lessons as well as breaks.
- Parents invited to see what children learn through play.
- Teachers sharing ideas in regular training sessions.

None of this needs a large budget. It needs a shared belief that play is part of learning, and spaces that support it.

## The role of the playground

The physical environment shapes what teachers can do. A bare concrete yard limits play-based learning to running games. A playground with climbers, balance trails, play panels, sand, planting and open space gives teachers far more to work with. See [how play-based learning can transform education in India](/blog/how-play-based-learning-can-transform-education-in-india.html) and [what every school playground should include](/blog/what-every-school-playground-should-include.html).

## Measuring progress

Schools can track simple indicators: how much time children spend outdoors each week, how often lessons use the outdoor space, teacher confidence with play-based methods, and children's engagement. Small, steady gains matter more than a single big change.
