Almost every serious playground injury involves a fall. The equipment above decides how far a child can fall; the floor below decides how hard they land. Getting the two to match is the single most important safety decision in a playground, and it is often the one decided last, when the budget is nearly spent.

## What fall height means

**Free fall height** is the distance from the highest point a child can stand, sit or hang on the equipment down to the surface below. On a play tower it is usually the floor of the highest platform. On a climbing net it is the highest foothold. On a swing it is the height of the seat at the top of its swing. Your supplier should give you the free fall height of every unit.

**Critical fall height** is a property of the surface. It is the highest fall the surface can absorb, in a standard drop test, without a high risk of serious head injury. Rubber, turf systems and loose fills are tested under EN 1177 (or ASTM F1292 in the US) to find this figure at a given thickness.

The rule is simple: **the surface's critical fall height must be at least the free fall height of the equipment above it.**

### Rubber tiles

Tiles are popular in schools and societies because they look tidy, drain well when laid on a sloped base, and can be replaced one at a time. Ask for the critical fall height at the thickness you are buying. Interlocking or bonded edges matter: a lifted corner is a trip hazard.

### Poured rubber (EPDM)

A shock-absorbing base layer is poured on site with a thinner coloured EPDM layer on top. It gives a seamless surface that can carry patterns, games and paths, which is why it suits [coloured play zones](/blog/coloured-playground-paths-and-zones-ideas.html). The base layer is made thicker under high equipment. The quality depends heavily on the installer, so ask to see their previous work.

### Artificial turf with a shock pad

Turf looks green all year and stays clean, but turf by itself absorbs very little. It must sit on a tested shock pad chosen for the fall height. In summer, turf can get warm; lighter colours and shade help.

### Sand

Sand is the cheapest surface that genuinely protects, but only when it is deep and looked after. A 300 mm layer that has been kicked down to 50 mm under a swing protects nobody. Plan for edging to keep it in, a drained base, regular raking and topping up.

## How far the surface should extend

The surface must cover the whole **impact area**, not just the footprint of the equipment. Under EN 1176 that is typically at least 1.5 m around most equipment, increasing for higher fall heights. Swings need much more space in the direction of travel, and slide exits need a clear run-out. Your layout drawing should show the impact area of each unit; our guide to [playground layout and safety zones](/blog/playground-layout-and-safety-zones.html) explains how to plan them.

<Tip title="Budget tip" text="You do not need the thickest surface everywhere. Use the full thickness under the tallest units and around swings, and a thinner, cheaper build-up under low equipment and on paths. Ask your supplier to zone the surface on the layout drawing." />

## The base matters as much as the top

Rubber laid on loose soil will sink and crack. In India the base also has to deal with monsoon rain. A good installation has:

- A compacted, level sub-base, or a concrete base for poured rubber and tiles.
- A slight fall (slope) so water runs off, and drainage at the low edge.
- Edging that holds loose fill or the edge of the rubber in place.

## Checking the surface over time

Surfaces wear fastest where children land and push off: under swings, at slide exits and below the climbing points. Check these spots every month, top up loose fill, and repair lifted tiles straight away.
