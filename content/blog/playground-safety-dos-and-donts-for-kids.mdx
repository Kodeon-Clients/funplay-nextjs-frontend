Most playground injuries are not freak accidents. They happen in a handful of predictable ways: a fall onto a hard surface, a collision near a moving swing, a toddler on equipment built for older children, or a broken part nobody reported. That is good news, because predictable injuries can be prevented with simple habits.

This guide has two parts. First, ten rules children can actually remember. Then the do's and don'ts for the adults who run the play area: parents, teachers, society committees and park staff.

## 10 playground safety rules for kids

Keep these short enough to say out loud and to print on a rules board. Pictures help younger children and anyone who does not read English.

1. **Play where a grown-up can see you.**
2. **Wait for your turn.** One child at a time on the slide and the swing.
3. **Slide down sitting up, feet first.** Never head first, never climb up the slide.
4. **Hold on with both hands** when you climb.
5. **Keep away from moving swings.** Walk around them, not in front or behind.
6. **No pushing, pulling or rough games** on the equipment.
7. **Wear closed shoes.** No loose strings, scarves or dupattas that can catch.
8. **Do not play on wet or very hot equipment.**
9. **Use the equipment meant for your age.**
10. **Tell an adult** if something is broken or if someone gets hurt.

## Do's for parents, teachers and park staff

### Check the area before children play

A thirty-second look prevents most problems. Walk around the equipment and look for loose bolts, cracked plastic, sharp edges, chains that are twisted or worn, and anything left on the ground: glass, stones, stray wires. After rain, check for puddles on platforms and slippery steps.

### Make sure the surface can take a fall

The ground under and around climbing frames, slides and swings should absorb impact. Rubber tiles, poured rubber (EPDM), artificial turf with a shock pad, or a deep layer of loose sand all work. Grass wears into hard soil within a few months of heavy use, and concrete or paver blocks should never sit under play equipment. Our guide to [school playground surface materials](/blog/school-playground-surface-materials-which-one-is-best.html) compares the options.

### Separate the age groups

Children under five need low platforms, small steps and slides they can manage on their own. Older children want height and challenge. When both share one structure, the little ones get knocked over and the older ones get bored and climb where they should not. Mark a separate toddler zone, even if it is only a few pieces of equipment. See [designing age-appropriate zones](/blog/designing-age-appropriate-zones-in-outdoor-playgrounds.html) for layouts.

### Supervise from where you can see everything

Benches and shade for adults should face the equipment, not the road or the clubhouse. In schools, place the play area where staff on duty can see all of it from one spot.

### Keep children hydrated and out of the hottest sun

In most of India, metal and dark plastic can get hot enough to burn skin on an April afternoon. Plan play for the morning and evening, provide shade where you can, and touch slides and platforms before small children use them.

## Don'ts

- **Don't let children play on wet equipment.** Steps, platforms and slides stay slippery for a while after rain.
- **Don't overcrowd one structure.** If twenty children are queuing for one slide, the play area is too small for the number of users. Add equipment or stagger break times.
- **Don't ignore small damage.** A cracked seat or a missing bolt becomes a serious hazard quickly. Take the item out of use and report it.
- **Don't let small children use outdoor gym equipment.** Fitness machines have moving parts and gaps sized for adults, not children.
- **Don't allow rough games on raised platforms.** Tag and chasing games belong on open ground, not on the structure.
- **Don't fix equipment with improvised parts.** Rope, wire and wrong-sized bolts create new hazards. Ask the supplier for proper spares.

## Rules boards that children actually read

A good rules board is short, pictorial and placed at the entrance at a child's eye level. Use the local language as well as English. Add the age range for the equipment, the timings, an emergency number and the contact for reporting damage. Six to ten rules is enough; a board with thirty lines will not be read by anyone.
