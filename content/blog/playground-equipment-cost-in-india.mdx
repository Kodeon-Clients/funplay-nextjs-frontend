"How much will a playground cost?" is the first question most committees ask, and the hardest to answer in one line. Two play areas that look similar in a brochure can be priced very differently, and both prices can be fair. This guide explains what drives the cost so you can plan a realistic budget and compare quotes properly.

## Where the money goes

## What makes the equipment itself cost more or less

### Play value and size

Price follows how much play a unit offers: how many children can use it at once, how many activities it has (slides, climbers, bridges, panels) and how tall the platforms are. A compact station for toddlers costs a fraction of a large multi-tower structure with tube slides and nets. Comparing price per child served is often more useful than comparing price per item.

### Materials and finish

The things that make equipment last are mostly invisible in a photo:

- **Steel:** pipe diameter and wall thickness, galvanising before painting, and the quality of the powder coat.
- **Plastic:** roto-moulded, UV-stabilised polyethylene of a good wall thickness fades and cracks far more slowly than thin or recycled plastic.
- **Wood:** species, treatment against termites and moisture, and stainless fixings.
- **Hardware:** stainless or galvanised bolts, sealed bearings on moving parts.

Read more in [choosing durable playground materials](/blog/choosing-durable-playground-materials.html).

### Standard or custom

Standard designs from a range are the most economical: the moulds, drawings and testing already exist. Custom colours cost a little more; custom themes, shapes and sizes cost more again, because they need design work and sometimes new moulds. See [customization options for themed playgrounds](/blog/customization-options-for-themed-playgrounds.html).

## Surfacing and civil work

Surfacing surprises many buyers. It must cover the full safety zone around each unit, which is usually several times the footprint of the equipment, and its thickness depends on the fall height. A good base with drainage adds cost but doubles the life of the surface. Our [fall height and safety surfacing guide](/blog/playground-fall-height-and-safety-surfacing-guide.html) explains how to size it, and how to zone it so the thickest build-up is only where it is needed.

## Site factors

- **Location and access:** distance from the factory, and whether a truck can reach the site or parts must be carried in.
- **Podium and terrace sites:** loads, waterproofing and fixings need extra care. See [terrace and podium play areas](/blog/terrace-and-podium-play-areas-for-high-rise-buildings.html).
- **Existing conditions:** removing old equipment, breaking concrete, or correcting levels.

## How to plan a budget

1. **Start with the users.** How many children, of what ages, at peak times?
2. **Measure the space** and mark the areas that are shaded, sunny and wet in the monsoon.
3. **Decide what you want to fund now** and what can wait. Phasing is fine; leave space and, ideally, foundations for phase two.
4. **Ask two or three suppliers** for a layout and a line-by-line quote for the same scope. Our [quote guide](/blog/quote-guide-for-playground-equipment-what-to-consider-before-purchasing.html) lists what to check.
5. **Keep a margin** for surfacing and civil work, which are the items most often underestimated.

<Tip title="Where not to save" text="Cut the number of pieces before you cut the quality of the surface or the steel. A smaller playground that is safe and still looks good after five years is better value than a large one that is rusting and closed in three." />

## Ways to stretch a tight budget

- Choose one high-play-value structure rather than many single items.
- Use standard designs and colours.
- Zone the surfacing by fall height.
- Add low-cost ground-level play: play panels, hopping posts, painted games on rubber.
- Plan a second phase for swings or a toddler zone.
