India has many playgrounds, but many children still cannot use one. Access is not only about how many playgrounds exist; it is about whether a particular child can reach one, feel safe there and find something they can play on.

## Close to home

Young children and many girls play mainly near home. A network of small play spaces in every neighbourhood reaches far more children than a single large park. See [from concrete to playgrounds](/blog/from-concrete-to-playgrounds-how-indian-cities-can-create-more-child-friendly-spaces.html).

## Safe and welcoming

Playgrounds that are overlooked, lit and busy with families feel safe for everyone, especially girls. Seating for parents and grandparents helps.

## Inclusive

Accessible paths and equipment that children of different abilities can share. See [inclusive playgrounds](/blog/growing-need-for-inclusive-playgrounds-in-indian-communities.html).

## Kept open

A broken playground is a closed playground. Regular inspection and repair keep access real. See our [inspection checklist](/blog/playground-safety-inspection-checklist.html).

## Rural and urban gaps

Access looks different in cities and villages:

- **In cities,** the problem is usually space and distance: playgrounds exist but are far away, crowded or behind a gate that only some children can pass.
- **In villages and small towns,** there is often open land but no safe equipment, and school grounds are bare. See [how playgrounds can improve child development in rural India](/blog/how-playgrounds-can-improve-child-development-in-rural-india.html).

Both need different answers: small, well-placed play spaces in cities, and durable, low-maintenance equipment in rural schools and community spaces.

## Making play spaces welcoming for girls

Girls in many parts of India have less freedom to roam than boys and stop using public play spaces at a younger age. Small design choices help:

- Play spaces close to home and on busy, overlooked routes.
- Good lighting for evening use.
- A mix of activities, not only one big structure dominated by older boys: swings, social seating, balance trails and games built into the floor.
- Seating for mothers and grandmothers, so the space feels like a family space.

## Who is responsible?

Access improves when everyone with a stake plays their part:

- **City authorities** plan, fund and maintain public playgrounds. See [planning a public park playground](/blog/planning-a-public-park-playground.html).
- **Builders** provide play areas inside every residential project.
- **Schools** improve their playgrounds and, where possible, open them after hours.
- **Companies** fund playgrounds in under-served areas through CSR.
- **Residents** look after their local play spaces and report damage.

No single group can provide play for every child, but together they can make sure every neighbourhood has somewhere safe to play.
