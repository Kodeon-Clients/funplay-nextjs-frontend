In many schools, playtime is seen as a break from learning. In fact it is part of it. What children do on the playground supports what they do in the classroom, and the playground itself can be a classroom.

## Physical foundations

Climbing, hanging, balancing and swinging build the core strength, shoulder stability and coordination that children need to sit well, hold a pencil and copy from the board. Children who move more often find fine motor tasks easier.

## Attention and self-regulation

After active play many children return to class calmer and more focused. Games with rules teach waiting, taking turns and controlling impulses. See [how physical play improves focus](/blog/how-physical-play-reduces-screen-time-and-improves-focus-in-students.html).

## Social and emotional skills

Group play involves negotiating, sharing, leading, following and resolving disagreements: skills that matter in every classroom.

## Learning through the playground

- **Maths:** counting steps, measuring slides, timing races, shapes in the equipment.
- **Science:** forces on a swing, friction on a slide, balance on a see-saw, plants and insects.
- **Language:** describing, storytelling, role play in themed structures.
- **Art:** colours, patterns, drawing the playground.

## Making it work in a busy timetable

- **Short and regular** works better than occasional long outdoor sessions. Ten minutes outside to measure or observe can anchor a classroom lesson.
- **Plan with the playground in mind.** Teachers who know what equipment exists can build it into lesson plans.
- **Agree simple rules** for outdoor lessons so they do not turn into free play unless intended.
- **Use shade and the cooler parts of the day** in summer.

## The playground as a school asset

A playground is often seen as a cost. Schools that use it for learning as well as breaks get far more value from it, and children see play and learning as connected.
