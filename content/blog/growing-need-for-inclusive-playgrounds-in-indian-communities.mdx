For many children with disabilities in India, the local playground is somewhere they watch from the side. Steps instead of ramps, loose surfaces a wheelchair cannot cross, and equipment that only works if you can climb all leave them out. Inclusive playgrounds change that, and they make the space better for everyone else too.

## What inclusive play means

Inclusive play is not a separate "special" corner. It means children of different abilities can play **together**. A child who uses a wheelchair can reach the play panels her friends are using; a child with autism has a quieter space to retreat to; a child with low vision can follow a contrasting path.

## Why it matters

- Children with disabilities have the same need to play, move and make friends.
- Shared play helps all children understand difference.
- Grandparents, carers with prams and parents with young children benefit from the same features.
- Public bodies increasingly expect accessible design in public spaces.

## Practical design steps

### Accessible routes and surfaces

Firm, level paths to and around the play area, and firm impact-absorbing surfaces (rubber or poured rubber) rather than loose sand where wheelchairs need to go.

### Ground-level play

Play panels, music elements, sand tables at accessible heights and roundabouts level with the ground let children join without climbing.

### Shared equipment

Nest swings, wide slides and large platforms allow friends and carers to join.

### Sensory variety

Sounds, textures, colours and quieter spaces suit children with different sensory needs.

### Rest and supervision

Seating with shade for carers, close to the play.

## Start where you are

You do not need to rebuild. An accessible path, a nest swing, ground-level panels and a firm surface around them can make an existing play area much more inclusive.
